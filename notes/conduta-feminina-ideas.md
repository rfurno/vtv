"Feminine Conduct" in the Huangdi Sijing (Four Political Treatises of the Yellow Emperor)
The book you referenced — Leo S. Chang and Yu Feng, The Four Political Treatises of the Yellow Emperor (University of Hawai'i Press, 1998) — is the standard English translation of the Huangdi Sijing (黃帝四經), the silk manuscripts discovered in 1973 at Mawangdui Tomb 3 near Changsha. These texts were found appended to a copy of the Daodejing on the same silk, and they're attributed to the legendary Yellow Emperor, reflecting the "Huang-Lao" syncretic tradition (Daoism + Legalism + military strategy).

The concept: masculine conduct vs. feminine conduct
The text establishes two fundamental modes of conduct for rulers:

Masculine conduct — aggressive, forceful, taking initiative
Feminine conduct — yielding, receptive, modest, acting second rather than first
The treatises repeatedly praise feminine conduct as the superior strategy. Key passages from Chang & Feng's translation (p. 105–106):

"If one never suffers ill-fortune it is because he holds fast to the feminine conduct."

"Anyone who is fond of applying masculine conduct should be called: one who is harmful to life."

"Anyone who is fond of applying feminine conduct should be called a recipient of emoluments. If he is now rich, he will become richer; if he is poor he will have food to eat. If he defends himself, his defense will be stable; if he acts he will succeed; if he seeks something he will surely obtain it; his struggle will overcome his enemy."

The text also notes that "losses suffered due to female conduct will result in future rewards… and it is called the accumulation of virtues to suffer losses by persisting in female conduct" and that "generally those who act first suffer bad fortune, while those who act later are assured of good fortune."

The critical nuance
Here's what makes this distinct from the Laozi: the Sijing advocates assuming feminine conduct as a strategic default, but the ruler switches to an active (masculine) posture at "the right moment" — countervailing against the Laozi's more thoroughgoing passivity. It's strategic yin, not absolute pacifism.

How this connects to your research thread
This is the textual bedrock beneath Galvany's "feminización de la guerra" argument. Galvany draws on this same tradition — the Yellow Emperor receiving strategy from a feminine figure, the Sijing's praise of feminine conduct — to argue that Sunzi's Art of War privileges traditionally feminine-coded qualities (softness, indirection, yielding, deception) over brute force. The Huangdi Sijing makes it explicit in political/strategic terms, while Sunzi applies it to the battlefield.

So you have a clean genealogy: Huangdi Sijing → Sunzi → Galvany's "feminización" reading, all rooted in the yin/yang cosmological framework where the feminine principle is strategically superior.

The Chang & Feng (1998) translation is the primary source for English-language engagement with this concept. Solid, citable, academically respectable.
