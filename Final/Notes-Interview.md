# Notes based on interview with Nataniel Rosa

## Context
Interessante esse paralelo. Isso que você falou com relação ao Sigung é muito interessante porque ele cunhou um nome que hoje nós concebemos como uma denominação, da qual a grande herança dela é um modo de acesso a esse sistema, que ele chamou de Kung Fu Life, que seria Vida Kung Fu. E eu sempre ouvi dizer que a linguagem da Vida Kung Fu era o silêncio. E isso sempre me chamou a atenção.  
E a primeira vez que eu fui pra Nova York, eu aprendi isso com ele.

Nós ficamos duas semanas. A viagem foi atípica porque era a aposentadoria dele e a festa de aniversário. Na segunda semana só ficaram eu e o Marcelo Abreu. Um dia o Sifu me chamou e falou: “Vou te orientar em uma coisa.”  

Ele tomava chá o tempo todo no Mo Kwoon. Me levou até o lugar onde preparavam o chá e disse que eu ficaria responsável por preparar o chá para ele.  

No dia seguinte, logo cedo, depois do café da manhã e do Yum Cha, fui para o Mo Kwoon e preparei o chá. Levei e deixei em cima da mesa dele. Ele nem olhou. O chá ficou lá. Depois de um tempo troquei por outro porque achei que estava frio. Ele nem tocou. No almoço ele estava tomando chá, mas não o meu. Voltei e fiz de novo. Nada. No dia seguinte repetiu-se a mesma coisa.  

Até que eu parei, observei e reparei: o chá que serviam no restaurante era bem mais claro do que o que eu estava fazendo. Eu estava fazendo quase um café para ele. Então voltei para o Mo Kwoon e preparei o chá clarinho, da mesma forma que vi no restaurante. Levei para ele e ele tomou.  

Nenhum momento ele reclamou. Nenhum momento ele falou “você tem que fazer assim, assim, assim”. Ele esperou que eu tomasse consciência. É a lógica de desenvolvimento. Quando o Sifu mostra uma vez a forma, ele não fica mandando você copiar e corrigindo o tempo todo. Ele espera que você desenvolva a percepção.  

Depois conversei com ele sobre isso. Foi uma lição. A partir dali o mundo fica mais alerta e pronto para receber. Cada experiência que tive com ele foi muito rica.

### Note 1: Ancestral Intelligence

**Ancestral Intelligence #1**

I used to think martial arts was just about fighting and feeling kick-ass powerful. My Kung Fu brother Nataniel Rosa thought the same when he was 15.

He trained every day and even became a state vice-champion… but he still felt like **the most insecure person in the world** — not just in fights, but with girls, friends, and life in general.

Only years later did he understand: real confidence isn’t just physical. It’s emotional. It’s about how you handle the real battles of everyday life.

Have you ever felt that gap between looking strong on the outside and feeling unsure on the inside?


### Note 2: No end to my own insecurities

When I reflect on my Kung Fu brother Nataniel’s words about overcoming insecurity, I think of my first Vedanta class with Acharya Jonas.

Even though I had trained in martial arts for years, I still carried deep insecurities. And as I started studying Vedanta, I realized that many of the troubles I had in life were related to other endless searches: for comfort, happiness, pleasure and growth. Those went far deeper than physical training or material possessions.

I had a new journey ahead: my internal journey to let go of those deep desires.

What illusion about yourself have you had to let go of?


### Note 3: Lessons Learned Through Silence

Ving Tsun Patriarch Moy Yat never yelled corrections. He taught in a much deeper way.

In New York, my Kung Fu brother Nataniel was tasked by our Sifu to prepare Sigung Moy Yat's tea every day. But Sigung wouldn’t drink it for days in a row. He didn’t complain — he just waited.

After several days, Nataniel finally noticed: the tea at the restaurant was very light. He made it the same way… and this time Sigung drank it.

No scolding. No direct orders.

The lesson? The master shows you once. Then he waits for you to **develop your own perception**.

Sometimes silence speaks louder than words.

What’s the best lesson you ever learned without anyone having to explain it?


### Note 4: Lessons Learned Through Silence - Vedanta Edition

After listening to Nataniel’s story about Sigung Moy Yat teaching through silence with the tea, I immediately thought of my own practice.

As soon as I finished my japamala, I used to get up and rush into the day. Acharya Jonas gently pointed it out in class one day:

“It’s like starting the car, turning on the radio, and then leaving the car behind to walk to work.”

That hit me hard.

I’m learning to stay in the silence after practice. When my mind becomes quiet, the real work begins — sorting through the clutter in my own mind’s drawers.

Sometimes the deepest lessons come not from words, but from what we notice in the silence.

Have you ever discovered something important about yourself only when you finally became quiet?


### Note 5: When the student is ready, the master appears

The relationship between Sifu and student isn’t always easy. It has ups and downs, like any relationship that really matters.

Nataniel Rosa shared that it took him years to understand: a true master doesn’t just train fighters — he helps shape **complete human beings**.

Today, as a family leader in the Ving Tsun community, he sees the responsibility on both sides: the student must be open to learn, and the Sifu must be ready to teach.

What do you think makes a great teacher-student relationship?

### Note 6: When the student is ready, the master appears - Vedanta Edition

I was attending Vedanta class for months already. Not much was moving inside of me.

I liked Acharya Jonas’ words and could repeat them easily, feeling like *I got it*.

But I wasn’t really there. Only when I decided to watch the classes with full attention — taking notes, thinking deeply about the lessons, and trying to apply them to daily life — did I finally welcome Acharya into my life as a true teacher.

When the student is truly ready… the master appears.

What have you done (or stopped doing) that finally opened you up to real learning?
