# The Remover of Darkness

## Illusion of Knowledge and the Two Clouds

In April 1900, Lord Kelvin delivered a lecture at the Royal Institution in London that has become legendary in the history of science. The published version opens with these words:

> “The beauty and clearness of the dynamical theory, which asserts heat and light to be modes of motion, is at present obscured by two clouds.”

By then, classical physics had achieved extraordinary success. Newtonian mechanics described the motion of bodies with precision. Maxwell’s equations unified electricity, magnetism, and light. Thermodynamics and the kinetic theory of gases offered powerful explanations for heat and energy. To many, the great map of physical reality was essentially complete. Only two small, irritating problems remained — two “clouds” on an otherwise clear horizon.

One concerned the mysterious ether through which light was thought to travel, and the failure of experiments to detect the Earth’s motion through it. The other was the ultraviolet catastrophe — the absurd prediction that a hot body should radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths.

Fix these minor details, the thinking went, and physics would be finished. Future work would consist mainly of measuring known constants to another decimal place.

Within five years those two clouds had become revolutions. Planck’s quantum hypothesis and Einstein’s 1905 papers on relativity and the photoelectric effect did not merely patch the old framework. They revealed that the framework itself rested on assumptions that could not survive contact with reality at its extremes. The obstacle had never been simple ignorance. It was the confident, well-earned illusion that the existing map already captured almost everything that mattered.

The real obstacle was never ignorance. It was the quiet, powerful illusion that they already knew almost everything that mattered.

I recognized the pattern in my own life long before I could name it.

From one direction I had doubled down on the STEM path. University classes in Physics, followed by a full-time IT job while still studying, gave me the satisfying sense that any problem could be broken down, analyzed, and solved with logic, data, and a cool head. The tools felt sufficient. The map felt reliable. Success would follow from clearer thinking and harder application.

From the other direction, life itself was accelerating. The income jump from intern to professional quickly became a high-speed treadmill. What had once felt like arrival — financial stability, technical competence, the outward markers of progress — turned into a relentless cycle of more: more responsibilities, more expectations, more sophisticated ways to stay ahead. The very competence that had seemed like freedom began to feel like a refined form of captivity.

I had my own two clouds. One was the belief that mastery of systems and information would eventually deliver control and lasting satisfaction. The other was the growing suspicion that the system I was mastering was quietly mastering me.

It took years — and eventually the encounter with living lineages in both Ving Tsun and Vedanta — to see that these were not separate problems. They were two faces of the same illusion: the confident assumption that the maps I had inherited or constructed were adequate to the territory of a human life.

## The Hurricane Phase

The clouds were no longer distant. They were picking up speed.
I watched wars play out on the news and long-time politicians trading accusations. I saw friends turn on each other over political or ideological lines. During the turmoil of 2020 I heard people demanding that others “trust the science” while others called politicians and officials heroes. The noise was constant, and it felt like violence was moving closer to everyday life.

At the same time, my life in South Florida offered easy escapes. There were parties, sun, beaches, and the endless distractions of Miami’s social scene. On the surface, things could still feel bright and easy.
Inside, though, something was shifting. My self-defense training was becoming more serious. I began studying law and the Constitution, hoping these tools would help me navigate an increasingly chaotic world and hold my own in arguments. I was building better maps — sharper analysis, stronger arguments, more reliable techniques.

Yet none of it seemed to quiet the growing storm. It felt like the hurricanes we get in South Florida: sudden, loud, and destructive. They tear through whatever looks stable — trees, roofs, even the fragile peace between neighbors fighting over a can of gas. The outer world was loud and unpredictable, but the louder storm was the one inside. Old patterns of reactivity, fear, and the need to be right were spinning faster. The more I tried to manage the chaos with better information or better technique, the more clearly I felt the limits of those tools.

The maps I had been relying on — whether professional, political, or martial — were starting to show their edges.
