# The Remover of Darkness
## Illusion of Knowledge





“The great obstacle to discovering the shape of the earth, the continents, and the ocean was not ignorance but the illusion of knowledge.”
Daniel Boorstin wrote that about explorers who kept sailing with maps they trusted too much.
What if the same trap sits in the training space or on the cushion?
You walk in already carrying ideas about what Ving Tsun or Vedanta is supposed to be. You’ve seen the clips, read a book or two, maybe even trained a bit. So when the correction lands or the question cuts deeper than expected, something in you pushes back — not because you’re lazy, but because the new information threatens the map you already have.
The practice doesn’t mainly need you to add more knowledge.
It needs you to notice which parts of what you “know” are actually in the way.
That’s the quiet work behind the four pillars I wrote about earlier — the transmission that only happens when both people are willing to let old pictures fall apart.
What’s one thing you were sure about in your own practice that later had to be revised or dropped? No performance. Just the honest version.



## Context

Here’s a clear, no-fluff breakdown of common misconceptions about Boorstin’s line.
The quote (from The Discoverers, 1983, and variations in his earlier essays) is powerful, but it’s often read in ways Boorstin didn’t intend — and sometimes in ways that weaken its usefulness for your VTV writing.
1. “Boorstin was saying most people in the past thought the Earth was flat”
This is the biggest and most widespread misreading.
Reality: Educated people in medieval Europe generally accepted a spherical Earth (following Greek thinkers like Aristotle and Eratosthenes). The dramatic “Christian dogma forced everyone to believe in a flat Earth until Columbus” story is largely a 19th-century myth used in science-vs-religion arguments. Boorstin leaned into this popular myth in parts of the book (especially the chapter “A Flat Earth Returns”), which historians have criticized him for. He overstated how widespread flat-Earth belief actually was and underplayed how much accurate ancient geography was still known.
Why it matters for you: If you open a Note with the quote, some readers will immediately think “flat Earth myth” and tune out or argue. You can still use the line, but it helps to quickly ground it in something less contested (e.g., wrong maps of continents, or rigid adherence to old authorities like Ptolemy or Galen in medicine).
2. “The quote means ignorance itself wasn’t a problem — only overconfidence was”
Reality: Boorstin isn’t saying ignorance is fine or neutral. He’s saying confident wrongness (the illusion of already having the correct map) is more dangerous because it actively stops people from looking further or testing their assumptions. Blank ignorance at least leaves room for curiosity. Pretending you already know closes that door.
Why readers get this wrong: The phrasing is memorable and slightly paradoxical, so people flip it into “ignorance is okay.” It’s not. The point is comparative: the bigger blocker was the illusion, not the lack of information.
3. “Boorstin was anti-tradition, anti-religion, or anti-authority in general”
Reality: He was criticizing rigid, untestable attachment to old authorities when new evidence appeared. He wasn’t against tradition or community transmission itself (in other parts of his work he writes positively about how knowledge gets passed and refined). He was against dogma that refused correction.
Why it matters for VTV: Your whole project is about living lineages and person-to-person transmission. You can actually use the quote in support of that — the danger is when students arrive with a fixed mental map of “what Ving Tsun is” or “what Vedanta should feel like” and can’t hear the correction. That’s the illusion, not the lineage itself.
4. “This only happened in the past — we’re past that now”
Reality: Boorstin saw the pattern as recurring across history (geography, astronomy, medicine, etc.). The same dynamic shows up today in overconfident paradigms in science, psychology, self-help, and even spiritual communities (“I already understand non-duality / flow state / structure” etc.).
Why readers miss this: The quote is usually presented in a historical context, so people treat it as a story about “them back then.” It’s more useful when treated as a timeless human tendency — including in personal practice.
5. “Boorstin was promoting radical skepticism or saying all knowledge is just illusion”
Reality: He was a strong believer in cumulative, evidence-based discovery and human progress through better observation and testing. He wasn’t a relativist or postmodernist. The goal wasn’t to tear down all maps — it was to keep testing and correcting them against reality.
Why it matters: If your Note makes it sound like “all our ideas are illusions, man,” you’ll lose the rigor and the “punk” spirit of independent judgment that your own writing usually carries. The healthy move is: keep using good tools (skepticism, observation, repetition) while staying willing to revise the map when it stops matching experience.
6. “Discovery is mainly about brave individuals throwing off old maps”
Reality: Boorstin also wrote about communities of knowledge, shared standards, and slow incremental work. The lone genius myth is not his main emphasis.

Bottom line for your Substack:
The quote is still excellent raw material — especially for a Note on unlearning in practice — but it carries some baggage. The strongest versions of it in your voice will:

Quickly move past the historical geography example.
Apply it directly to the student’s or practitioner’s mental map (“what I already ‘know’ about correct structure / witnessing / transmission”).
Keep the balance: we’re not ditching rigor or lineage; we’re noticing when our current map is blocking the next correction.



Boorstin’s critique of mass media is mostly contained in his 1961 book The Image: A Guide to Pseudo-Events in America. It’s one of his sharpest and most prescient works, and it connects directly to the “illusion of knowledge” idea we’ve been discussing.
Core argument
Boorstin argued that modern media (especially the explosion of photography, film, radio, and television — what he called the Graphic Revolution) didn’t just report reality. It increasingly manufactured a synthetic version of it. People were becoming more immersed in images and second-hand representations than in direct, unmediated experience. The result was a culture full of confident but often shallow or distorted pictures of the world.
He wasn’t primarily angry at “bias” or “fake news” in the modern sense. He was diagnosing a deeper structural problem: the media’s need for constant content had turned news into a kind of performance.
Key concepts
1. Pseudo-events
These are events that are planned or staged primarily so they can be reported. They are not spontaneous happenings that the media then covers. Instead, they are created for the media.
Characteristics of a pseudo-event (Boorstin’s definition):

It is not spontaneous — someone planned or planted it.
It is designed mainly to be reported or photographed.
Its relationship to the underlying reality is ambiguous.
It is often self-perpetuating (the coverage itself becomes part of the story and generates more coverage).

Classic examples from his era: press conferences, photo opportunities, “leaks,” celebrity interviews, political spectacles staged for television, and “Man of the Year” type lists.
Modern equivalents are obvious: viral challenges, influencer “day in the life” videos, political rallies timed for maximum clips, controversy manufactured for engagement, and almost anything labeled “content.”
2. The rise of the Image over reality
Boorstin claimed we had shifted from living in a world of direct experience to living in a world of images about experience. We consume polished representations (photos, headlines, soundbites) more than we encounter the messy, unscripted thing itself. Over time, the image starts to feel more real than reality.
3. Celebrity vs. Hero
One of his most quoted lines: A celebrity is “a person who is known for his well-knownness.”

Heroes were known for great deeds or character.
Celebrities are known because the media machine has made them visible.
The media doesn’t just report fame — it manufactures and sustains it.

4. News as self-fulfilling and entertaining
Because media outlets need fresh material every day (or every hour), they fill the gaps with pseudo-events. This blurs the line between reporting and creating. The public then consumes these images as if they were straightforward reality, which shapes what people believe is important or true.
How this connects to the “illusion of knowledge” quote
They are two sides of the same observation.
The earlier quote was about how confident but wrong mental maps block real discovery more than simple ignorance. In The Image, Boorstin shows one powerful modern machine that creates and spreads those confident but often synthetic maps: mass media. People feel informed because they’ve consumed a steady diet of images and pseudo-events, but the knowledge is frequently shallow, second-hand, and shaped by what is easy to photograph or sensationalize.
This is why the book still feels relevant. Social media, YouTube, TikTok, and algorithmic feeds have massively accelerated exactly what Boorstin described.
Strengths of his critique

Extremely prescient. Many people today read The Image and say it predicted influencers, viral outrage cycles, and politics-as-spectacle better than most later writers.
It gives useful language (“pseudo-event”) for talking about manufactured content without having to call everything “fake.”
It ties media critique to a larger cultural diagnosis rather than just partisan complaining.

Limitations and fair criticisms

It can feel somewhat nostalgic for a pre-media golden age of “authentic” experience that probably never existed in pure form.
Boorstin sometimes underplays how active and critical audiences can be. Later media studies (especially in the 1970s–80s) pushed back by showing that people don’t just passively absorb images.
Written in 1961, it obviously doesn’t address the internet, user-generated content, or algorithmic amplification. Some critics say it’s too focused on top-down media manipulation and misses bottom-up dynamics.
At times his tone can come across as culturally conservative or elitist — wary of popular culture and visual media in general.

Relevance to your VTV Substack
This material is very usable for you.
The modern flood of “kung fu knowledge” and “Vedanta wisdom” on social media and YouTube is full of pseudo-events: perfectly lit technique demos, short “mind-blowing” explanations, staged “spontaneous” insights, and viral clips designed to generate engagement rather than transmit depth. These create exactly the illusion of knowledge you’ve been exploring — people feel they understand something because they’ve consumed the image of it.
A Note could work well here: contrast the pseudo-event nature of most online martial/spiritual content with the slow, embodied, person-to-person transmission you wrote about in Article 1. The “illusion of knowledge” quote + the media critique together make a strong one-two punch.



Yes — that point is worth unpacking because it shows Boorstin wasn’t a blanket critic of tradition or transmission. He had a clear distinction between rigid, untestable dogma (which he attacked) and living, self-correcting communities and institutions that allow knowledge to be passed on, tested, and refined across generations (which he often praised).
Here’s the elaboration, drawn from his major works:
In The Discoverers (the book with the “illusion of knowledge” quote)
Boorstin repeatedly shows discovery as a cumulative, collective process rather than the work of isolated geniuses. He has an entire section titled something like “Widening the Communities of Knowledge.”
Key positive themes:

Incremental building on the past: Discoveries don’t usually overthrow everything that came before. One generation improves, corrects, or extends the work of previous ones. He contrasts this slow compounding favorably with revolutionary ideologies that want to start from scratch.
Communities of time and feedback: He writes about how “communities of time” create the conditions for real progress. Explorers, for example, needed the ability to come home and share what they learned (what he calls “feedback”). Without institutions and networks that could receive, test, and preserve new information, individual discoveries stayed isolated and often useless.
Institutions matter: He gives credit to universities, scientific societies, correspondence networks (the old “republic of letters”), printing, and libraries as structures that made knowledge cumulative and correctable. These systems allowed ideas to be challenged, refined, and passed forward rather than frozen in authority.

In short, when the mechanisms for transmission included accountability, testing against reality, and the ability to correct errors, Boorstin saw them as one of humanity’s great achievements.
In his work as Librarian of Congress (The Republic of Letters)
Later in life, while running the Library of Congress, Boorstin spoke and wrote warmly about books and printed knowledge as a cumulative inheritance. He noted that in the age of the book, new works generally added to rather than replaced the old ones. Knowledge was understood as something that grew across generations through reading, writing, and shared conversation — not something reinvented every season.
He valued the “republic of letters” — that informal international community of scholars and writers who debated, corresponded, and refined ideas across borders and decades.
How this contrasts with The Image
This positive view makes his sharp critique in The Image more interesting, not contradictory.
In The Image, modern mass media (especially visual and broadcast media) often short-circuits the very processes Boorstin admired:

Pseudo-events replace spontaneous or hard-won experience.
Speed and visual appeal replace slow testing and feedback.
Celebrity and image-making replace reputation earned through sustained contribution.
The constant flood of new images and manufactured content makes cumulative, generational knowledge harder to maintain.

So Boorstin could celebrate careful, accountable transmission in one book and warn against its degradation by media-driven image culture in another.
Why this matters for your VTV project
This nuance is useful because your Substack is built on exactly the kind of transmission Boorstin sometimes praised: person-to-person lineages (Sifu-Todai, Acharya-Shishya), repetition over time, embodied feedback, and knowledge that is tested in practice rather than consumed as images.
You can use Boorstin in two complementary ways:

The “illusion of knowledge” quote + The Image critique → to show how modern media and quick online content create synthetic maps that block real learning.
His more positive writing on communities of knowledge → to highlight what makes living lineages valuable: they (ideally) preserve the conditions for correction, depth, and cumulative refinement that mass media often erodes.

It lets you position Ving Tsun and Vedanta practice as part of an older, slower, more accountable tradition of knowing — without having to treat Boorstin as purely negative.

The quote (commonly attributed to Bruce Lee, though it echoes older Zen and Taoist ideas) is usually phrased like this:
“Before I studied the art, a punch was just a punch.
After I studied the art, a punch was no longer just a punch.
Now that I understand the art, a punch is just a punch again.”
Why it works so well for your Substack
It maps almost perfectly onto the “illusion of knowledge” territory we’ve been exploring with Boorstin, Pirsig, and the Jonas Masetti material.
Here’s the connection:

Stage 1 (Beginner): A punch is just a punch. Simple, direct, no conceptual overlay. You haven’t built an elaborate mental model yet.
Stage 2 (Intermediate / “more than a punch”): This is where the illusion of knowledge kicks in hard. You now have technique, power generation, timing, strategy, biomechanics, mental game, lineage theory, etc. The punch becomes loaded with concepts, corrections, and “correct” ways of doing it. This stage is necessary, but it’s also where people can get stuck in their own mental maps — exactly what Boorstin warned about. The confident knowledge becomes the obstacle to direct action.
Stage 3 (Advanced): A punch is just a punch again — but now with depth. The concepts have been internalized or dropped. What returns is simplicity, but it’s a simplicity on the other side of complexity. This is the stage where real transmission and long sadhana show their value. It echoes Pirsig’s return to direct Quality and the Jonas notes on moving through the “hurricane” until you regain inner conviction and natural action.

This progression is very close to what you’re already writing about in the Ving Tsun + Vedanta space: the movement from external knowledge and conditioning → the necessary deconstruction → a more direct, embodied knowing.
