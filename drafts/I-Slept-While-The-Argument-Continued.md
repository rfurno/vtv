# I Slept While the Argument Continued
# What Changed When I Stopped Managing Other People’s Minds

I used to go to sleep still arguing.

Hours of research. Drafts of replies full of data. Coming to bed at 2 a.m., trying not to wake my wife. She had grown tired of calling me. I only needed a few more minutes at the keyboard.

The satisfaction of being right was addictive. Support would arrive in the feed. It felt like belonging to something larger.

Meanwhile I hadn’t called my parents in Brazil in weeks.

## The Slow Noticing

It began as a low-grade discomfort.

My wife asked me not to start another debate as we prepared to receive neighbors for breakfast.

The late nights still happened, but they started to feel more expensive. The research still felt urgent, yet the urgency itself was beginning to tire me. I would notice the pull to open another tab or draft another reply and, for a second, see it as a pull rather than a duty.

The same pattern showed up in ordinary conversation. Someone would mention something recent and the old machinery would start. I would interrupt them mid-sentence, already certain I had the better version of the story. The quiet satisfaction of being the one who saw more clearly.

Only now there was a small gap between the impulse and the action. In that gap I could feel how much energy the whole project was costing.

I still argued. I still researched. I still went to bed late. But the story I told myself about why it mattered was beginning to fray. The sense of being on a team, of fighting for something larger, no longer covered the simpler fact: my attention was almost always pointed outward, and the people closest to me were receiving what was left.

The small pause that came from letting someone finish — from receiving instead of preparing the reply — was starting to feel better than the old satisfaction of being right.

## Freedom Only When Others Are Free

The discomfort kept returning until one of the Vedanta classes gave it a clear shape.

On one side I want to be free. On the other I keep trying to control people — their opinions, their conclusions, the version of the story they are allowed to hold. What I understood from Acharya Jonas’s teaching was simple and hard: I cannot be free while I refuse to let others be free.

Something in me recognized it immediately. The late-night research, the perfect counter-narrative, the need to interrupt, the quiet lift of being the one who saw more clearly — all of it was a form of control. My energy was pointed outward, managing other minds instead of attending to my own life.

Then came a harder recognition.

My father was approaching his ninetieth birthday. His health was slowly declining. During a FaceTime call I saw his smile open as we talked about projects he and I used to do together — a smile I had not seen in months. I had been too busy winning arguments with strangers.

If so much of my attention was spent correcting, arguing, and sorting the world into better and worse versions of the truth, what was left for the people who actually shared my days — and for the work that was mine to do?

## What the Change Looks Like

The old pull has not disappeared.

I still feel it when someone says something I find incomplete or wrong. The counter-argument still forms. The small urge to correct, to complete the picture, to be the one who saw more clearly, still rises. What has changed is how often I obey it.

In conversation I now wait more. I let the other person finish. Sometimes the perfect reply I was preparing is no longer needed — the talk has already moved on. Other times I simply choose not to send the message I spent twenty minutes drafting.

Acharya Jonas spoke of *dama* — not the rejection of pleasure or impulse, but the capacity to feel the attraction and still choose not to act. The difference between automatic reaction and a conscious response.

That capacity is beginning to show up in ordinary life. I scroll past complaint pieces and hero-villain stories more quickly. I notice the old charge and, more often than before, put the phone down. 

At home the change is quieter still. My wife is happier when I come to bed without the laptop. Some nights I read a chapter out loud to her and keep going until she falls asleep.

I still fail at this regularly. The gap between impulse and action is real, but it is not always wide enough. Some days the old machinery runs before I notice it. The difference is that I now recognize the cost more quickly, and I am less willing to pay it.

## Respecting the Centerline
In Ving Tsun, Sifu Leo taught me to respect the centerline while practicing with a younger Kung Fu brother, a Si Dai. In Daan Chi Sau, for example, the Si Dai’s hand stretches out to meet mine on the centerline. That hand has its own path. My job is not to push it aside to claim dominance. I meet what is there, feel the direction, and work from that reality.

For a long time I failed to apply the same principle outside the Mo Goon.

I would meet someone’s opinion the way a beginner meets force — by trying to overpower it with a better argument, a clearer narrative, a more complete set of facts. I treated other people’s views as something to correct rather than something to receive first.

There is an important difference I had overlooked.

When I train with a Si Dai, he has already placed himself in the position of student. By showing up and putting out his arms, he has asked for assistance and feedback. The relationship is explicit. **The consent is clear.**

My neighbor at breakfast never asked for a lecture. Neither did the person across the table, or the stranger in a comment thread. They were not my students. They had not invited me to reshape their understanding.

Respecting the centerline in the Mo Goon was never only about technique. It was practice in meeting another person where they actually are, instead of where I thought they should be. I am only beginning to bring that same discipline into ordinary conversation.

## What Remains
The arguments still exist. People still hold views I find incomplete or wrong. The old machinery still starts from time to time. What has changed is the amount of my life I am willing to hand over to them.

I no longer treat every disagreement as a problem I am responsible for solving. I no longer treat every conversation as a chance to correct the record. The energy that used to go into managing other people’s minds is slowly returning to the people who actually share my days, and to the work that is mine.

I cannot be free while I refuse to let others be free. That sentence continues to work on me. It is not a conclusion I have finished living. It is a direction I am still learning to follow.

Some nights I still feel the pull to open the laptop and keep arguing. More often now, I leave it closed. I pick up a book. I read out loud until the room grows quiet.

The question that remains is simple.
What part of the story is actually mine?
