# The Diagnosis Came in July
# What the Teaching Could Not Soften

Jack was fourteen. The diagnosis came in July.

Acharya Jonas has said many times that the path is not a cave, not the couch, and not the ability to use the right technical terms. Learning happens at work, with the angry neighbor, with the needy friend, with the parent who brings out the worst of my rage, and when the body of the animal who lived with me for eleven years begins to fail.

When the veterinarian came into the waiting room to give us time to say goodbye, I felt a sudden rage at the story my intellect tried to push on me: it’s only a form dissolving.

I wanted the brain pulled out through the hole in my chest. The air gone, as if I'd been hit hard and couldn't breathe.

The teaching didn’t fill the hole. It just made the story my mind told me sound hollow.

## What Stays When the Form Goes

My sister's kids looked smaller once they were past the security checkpoint. From that distance I watched Melissa, still feisty, run back to her older brother after they had briefly separated.

I turned to my wife, both of us with tears, and said we would never see those little children again. In a year or two they would still be Lukas and Melissa, but older. Different bodies, different ways of moving, different voices.

The faces we associated with that Christmas would already be gone.

I still felt the need to hold on to them. To miss them. To want one more minute.

That’s the gap: what I’ve heard vs. what I feel.


## The Forgotten Tool

I am used to fixing things. Over the years I collected methods, frameworks, and arguments. Some of them worked. Most of them were just more tools.

One of the oldest ones came from my grandmother. When I was small enough to sleep in her bed on weekend visits, she taught me to pray.

In college my ego pushed it aside. Anything that could not pass the scientific method felt suspect. I stopped. I preferred the feeling of solving things myself.

The tool did not return on its own. It took something I could not fix.

## The Altar

The tool did not return as an idea. It returned as a daily action.

Acharya Jonas had repeated a pragmatic suggestion many times: throughout history certain forms of knowledge have been passed down and used, even without full scientific explanations. Some are called superstitions, others traditional knowledge. Why not test them directly?

It felt strange kneeling beside my wife before a makeshift altar on a side table: some flowers, a picture of Jack, a small figure of Ganesha. I was afraid she would think I was desperate. She would have been right.

I began doing puja every morning. The voice of my mantra instructor returned: “Just deliver it. With all your will.”

Then came the pause to decide what to ask. The answer was already in the words I had heard many times in class but had never used:

I asked for clarity and discernment. Not for the pain to disappear. Not for a better story to tell myself. Only for the ability to see more clearly what was already there.

Some days the practice helped. Some days it did nothing. The hole didn’t close on schedule. But the days started fitting together differently around that hole.


## The Form

One morning, while we were outside collecting flowers for the puja, the memory of my wife's nieces returned — a later Christmas, the same small figures who would also grow and change. This time the memory moved something. I could still call them on FaceTime, see their older faces, hear the different voices and laughter.

As I looked at the backyard where the children and Jack once wandered, my chest tightened again. I am left with my own limited form. I cannot know what lies ahead or where Jack’s form went. The intellect was still fighting. I am still learning it. Slowly.

When it was time, during the puja, to say what I was grateful for, the words came without effort:

I was grateful for the years with Jack. The warm, limited form was already gone. Something of the time with him remained.

Goodbye, Jack.
