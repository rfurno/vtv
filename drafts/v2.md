# The Remover of Darkness

## Illusion of Knowledge and the Two Clouds

In April 1900, Lord Kelvin delivered a lecture at the Royal Institution in London. The published version opens with these words:

> “The beauty and clearness of the dynamical theory, which asserts heat and light to be modes of motion, is at present obscured by two clouds.”

By then, classical physics had achieved extraordinary success. Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell’s equations, and thermodynamics formed a powerful framework. To many, the great map of physical reality was essentially complete. Only two small problems remained.

One concerned the mysterious ether and the failure to detect the Earth’s motion through it. The other was the ultraviolet catastrophe — the absurd prediction that a hot body should radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths.

Fix these minor details, the thinking went, and physics would be finished.

Within five years those two clouds had become revolutions. Planck’s quantum hypothesis and Einstein’s 1905 papers did not merely patch the old framework. They revealed that the framework itself rested on assumptions that could not survive contact with reality at its extremes. The obstacle had never been simple ignorance. It was the confident illusion that the existing map already captured almost everything that mattered.

I recognized the same pattern in my own life.

From one direction, I had doubled down on the STEM path. University classes in Physics, followed by a full-time IT job while still studying, gave me the satisfying sense that any problem could be broken down and solved with logic and analysis. The tools felt sufficient. From the other direction, life was accelerating. What had once felt like arrival quickly became a high-speed treadmill of more responsibilities and more sophisticated ways to stay ahead. The very competence that had seemed like freedom began to feel like a refined form of captivity.

I had my own two clouds. One was the belief that mastery of systems and information would eventually deliver control and lasting satisfaction. The other was the growing suspicion that the system I was mastering was quietly mastering me.

It took years — and eventually the encounter with living lineages in both Ving Tsun and Vedanta — to see that these were not separate problems. They were two faces of the same illusion: the confident assumption that the maps I had inherited or constructed were adequate to the territory of a human life.

## The Hurricane Phase

The clouds were no longer distant. They were picking up speed.

I watched wars unfold on the news and longtime politicians trading accusations. I saw friends turn on each other over ideology. During the turmoil of 2020 the noise grew louder — people demanding trust in one version of events while calling others heroes or villains. It felt like violence was moving closer to everyday life.

At the same time, South Florida offered easy escapes. Parties, sun, beaches, and the endless distractions of Miami’s social scene kept the surface bright and busy. On the outside, life could still feel light.

Inside, though, something was shifting. My self-defense training was becoming more serious. I started studying the Constitution and constitutional law, hoping these tools would help me navigate the chaos. I was building what felt like better maps.

Yet none of it quieted the growing storm. It reminded me of the hurricanes we get in South Florida — violent, loud, and destructive. They tear through whatever looks stable: roofs, trees, even the fragile peace between neighbors fighting over a can of gas. The outer world was unpredictable, but the louder storm was the one inside. Old patterns of reactivity, fear, and the need to be right were spinning faster. The more I tried to manage everything with better information or better technique, the more clearly I felt the limits of those tools.

I could see the manufactured drama on screens and the way conversations were being shaped. It felt like I was both watching and being watched in a kind of digital carnival. I was speeding toward nowhere, leaving the most important people behind — my wife, my parents, my family, and ultimately myself. The maps I had been relying on — professional, political, or martial — were leading me toward consumption, polarization, and quiet dependency.

At some point my wife invited me to take meditation classes and listen to lectures from a Buddhist monk. It helped. For the first time I had something that spoke directly to the storm instead of just trying to manage the external chaos. But even then, something still felt missing. The practices were helpful, but they were mostly self-directed. I was still largely on my own with the hurricane.

It slowly became clear that I had been looking in the wrong direction. The maps I trusted weren’t reaching the real storm. I had to turn inward.

What I didn’t know yet was that the real shift would come from living transmission — from someone who could see further than I could and help remove the darkness I was still mistaking for light.

## The Rope and the Snake

Up to that point I had been treating knowledge the way I treated most things: as something I could collect. More information, more techniques, more frameworks. I believed that if I just gathered enough, the storm inside would eventually settle. I already understood from Ving Tsun that real understanding does not come from piling up techniques. It comes through living transmission — through someone who has walked the path showing you how to walk it. But I had never brought that understanding into what was happening inside me.

On the way back from a trip, my wife was listening to her Vedanta class in the car. I heard Acharya Jonas speak, and something shifted. He was talking about Arjuna’s dilemma in the Bhagavad Gita. On the battlefield, faced with the prospect of fighting his own family and teachers, Arjuna drops his bow in despair. He is overwhelmed — not by lack of skill, but by inner conflict. That image stayed with me. I recognized the feeling.

I had heard about the Gita before, but this time it landed differently. I began to understand that Acharya Jonas was not just offering ideas. He was pointing to knowledge that is passed directly from one person to another.

That was when the light came on.

In Vedanta there is a simple image: a man walks along a dark path and sees what he takes to be a snake. He stops, frozen in fear. Then someone brings a light, and he sees it was only a coiled rope all along. Once he sees the rope, the fear does not return, even when the light is gone. The illusion has been seen through.

For a long time I had been studying the snake. I was trying to figure out what kind it was, what to do if it struck, how to protect myself. I was looking for better information and better techniques. What I actually needed was someone to shine a light on what I was really afraid of.

This did not happen through a video or a single powerful sentence. It took months of showing up to class, of listening, and of having my ideas gently taken apart and examined. I started to see something I had only heard before: we can only accept a teaching as far as the mind is ready to receive it. When there is a gap between what is being pointed to and what the mind can currently hold, that gap itself becomes the place where real learning can happen. But the mind cannot be forced. It has to be led, slowly, until it can see for itself.

I began to notice that most of what I had been calling “knowledge” was only information my mind had accepted. And anything I could not truly see and verify for myself had very little power to bring lasting peace. The more I tried to manage the storm with thinking, planning, or collecting better tools, the more clearly I felt how limited those tools were when the storm was inside.

It was the first time I understood that the remover of darkness is not a collection of ideas. It is a living relationship that can show you what you are still mistaking for a snake — and stay with you long enough for that seeing to become your own.

## Depending on the System

Moy Yat once said, “Kung Fu without a system is not good Kung Fu. Kung Fu that depends on a system is not good Kung Fu.”

The first part is easy to accept. Without structure, repetition, and transmission, there is nothing solid to practice. The second part is harder. Dependence on the system can quietly become another form of the illusion I had been living inside for years.

There is a progression that shows up across traditions. At first, a technique is simply what it appears to be. After study begins, it becomes loaded with concepts, corrections, intention — and, if we’re honest, ego. Later, after these layers have been absorbed and then allowed to fall away, the technique can return to something simpler, but now informed by everything that came before. Most of us spend a long time in the middle stage. This is where the system is most useful, but also where it is easiest to become dependent on it.

What I had understood for years in Ving Tsun, I was now slowly applying to what I was hearing in Vedanta. I had to learn to shine the light on my own path. Sometimes that light came through Acharya’s teachings. Sometimes through the various sadhanas. Sometimes through service to others in the community. I still don’t have a single name in Vedanta for what I call “Kung Fu Life,” but this is where the real work began.

Repetitive, embodied practice is not about adding more knowledge or becoming more skilled in the usual sense. It is the slow, often uncomfortable work of letting the one who is constantly managing, correcting, and measuring gradually lose its grip. In the beginning, this can feel like losing something. Over time, it becomes a kind of freedom.

Even then, the old patterns can return with force. I still get easily triggered by political news or conflict. Old fears, projections, and the need to control rise up, and the mind loses its bearings. In those moments, neither technique nor intellectual understanding is enough. What remains is śraddhā — a trust that is not blind, but steady enough to keep returning to practice even when nothing feels clear. It is the quiet conviction that the process is still moving, even when the storm makes it impossible to see the path ahead.

What changed was no longer trying to solve the storm alone with better thinking or stronger technique. Instead, I began to stay with it — through repetition, through relationship, and through the simple fact that I no longer had to figure everything out by myself. I was in good company.
