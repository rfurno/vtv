# The Little Idea: When Kung Fu Becomes Sadhana

**Purification, repetition, and the slow disappearance of the doer**

Siu Nim Tau — the “Little Idea” form — was the first thing my Sifu taught me in Ving Tsun. For hours I stood almost completely still, cycling one arm, then the other, then both. No kicks. No steps. Nothing resembling the explosive Bruce Lee sequences I had fantasized about since my early twenties.

What began as a brutal test of patience slowly revealed something deeper. First came the ability to visualize the movement before any muscle fired. Then arrived the quiet, almost uncomfortable realization: this was purification. I was burning away the childish hunger to leap through the air and knock an opponent out the way the movies had promised. The fantasy had to die.

In the etymology of **Kung Fu** (功夫), this is exactly what **Kung** (功) points to — the consummation of a feat through disciplined effort, the marriage of skillful work (**工**) and raw energy (**力**). **Fu** (夫) adds the mature, heroic character who wields that skill with moral clarity instead of ego. Not a flashy fighter. A mature practitioner.

This is where my two ancestral roads — one from the Chinese martial lineage, the other from Vedanta — began to speak to each other inside my own body and mind. Not as a blended system. Simply as parallel disciplines that kept forcing me to confront the same question, over and over: *Who is the one doing this?*

## The Long Road of Repetition

From my early twenties until now, both practices have operated the same way on me: relentless, often boring repetition that slowly dismantles the illusion that I’m in the driver’s seat.

In the kwoon there were no mirrors. Siu Nim Tau forced me to look inside for correct posture instead of chasing external feedback. My fantasies of heroic performance gradually burned off. On the Vedanta side, Acharya Jonas prescribed the same medicine: six months to a year of japa mala before anything else. No secret mantras, no shortcuts — just daily repetition until the mind began to settle.

The “Little Idea” was never little. It was the seed of a much larger process: the gradual purification of the instrument (body and mind) so something clearer could be seen. Both traditions demanded I show up daily and do the unglamorous work long after the initial excitement had worn off.

I’ll explore what that “myself” ultimately turned out to be in a future piece. For now, the point is simpler: real transformation only begins once the seeker is willing to do the boring thing, repeatedly, without applause — abandoning the need to be special.

## When the Doer Starts to Dissolve

Here’s where the practices converge most sharply.

In Acharya Jonas’ classes, action performed from ego-desire carries its own poison. But when the same action is offered without attachment — simply following the harmony of the moment — it purifies. Siu Nim Tau slowly became exactly that: movement arising, centerline held, arms cycling, while the sense of a separate “doer” grew thinner. The body knew the next position; the mind was no longer directing, only witnessing.

The same dissolution appears in the 108 movements of Muk Yan Jong — the Wooden Dummy form. There is no partner, yet the dummy demands precise, continuous contact. Any urge to hit it harder, faster, or with more power instantly exposes the ego at work. The same pattern showed up on the japa mala: I would finish a round feeling quietly proud, only to catch my mind rehearsing a clever comeback to an argument I’d had with my wife minutes earlier. Both Sifu Leo and Acharya Jonas would point it out immediately — something no TikTok tutorial or “secret mantra” video could ever do.

After thousands of repetitions on the dummy and on the beads, the form stops being something I’m “doing.” The movements begin to flow through the structure, and the centerline becomes the quiet axis **along which** everything moves. It starts to feel like I’m simply the observer of my thoughts and actions.

This is the real end game. Not better fighting. Not better meditation. Not even “spiritual martial artist” credentials. Just the quiet, sometimes brutal erosion of the seeker himself.

## Try This at Home

Stand relaxed with your arms at your sides, eyes closed. Take a few moments to scan your body — face, neck, shoulders, torso, legs — and release any unnecessary tension.

Without moving your hands, clearly visualize the palms turning forward while still resting at your sides.

Now let the hands slowly follow that visualization. Take a full minute (or longer) to complete the tiny movement. No rushing. If the arms jump ahead, jerk, or the mind starts narrating or correcting, simply notice it and begin again.

This is the very first movement of Siu Nim Tau. The point isn’t to do it perfectly. It’s to feel who you think is moving — and to let the body eventually move by itself while the mind watches.

Do this once a day for a week. Nothing fancy. Just repetition and honest observation. I’d love to hear what you notice in the comments.
