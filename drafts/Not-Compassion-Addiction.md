# Not Compassion, Addiction
## When I Mistook Being a Keyboard Warrior and Couch Potato for Compassion

Compassion is not pity, nor suffering along with the other. It is high life energy.

That's the latest invitation to dive into yet another corner of my life to remove that stinky trash I let accumulate over years.

For years I let myself be persuaded by social media into thinking that performing outrage, performing grief, performing solidarity was moral work.

Readers of "The Remover of Darkness" have met this man already — doom-scrolling through manufactured outrage, half-watching himself get played. That was before Vedanta. Back then I could see the trap. Counting what it cost me took a few more years.

Social platforms engineered the sensation of compassion — the spike of righteous energy, the dopamine of the dunk, the ritual of shared grief when I picked just the right whatsapp sticker for the occasion — without spending a drop of real life energy.

That's why it's addictive: all the feeling, none of the doing. High arousal, low energy.

I went looking for how many data points the platforms collect about me. Turns out they don't need the count for surveillance's sake — they instrument my emotional states. Dwell time on a distressing post, rage-taps, the hesitation before I shared outrage — also known as my "keyboard warrior" mode. They weren't accidentally corrupting my compassion; they were measuring it, millisecond by millisecond, and feeding me more of whatever kept me scrolling. I know my numbers. The post that made me angriest was the one I lingered on longest.

Then I looked around. I was still sitting on the same chair. The trash can next to me was filled with wrappers of sugary food. The world around me was still the same. Perhaps just with a few more annoyed people after I pushed the reply button.

## Off the Chair

I'd be lying if I wrote that I went to the local shelter to do voluntary work.

I found someone who needed more of my time and attention. The person spending his life and energy away lost in the whirlpool of bytes.

I remember that night: I deleted whatsapp, instagram, twitter, facebook, and a few other apps. Then time stopped leaking — more productive hours, or just less distress running in the background of my brain; I honestly can't tell. Either way, I could finally decide which unfinished projects to throw away and which ones to finish.

One by one, the attention drains were dismantled: I didn't waste three hours on youtube with a distracting stream of funny videos. Didn't judge the latest post of a 3rd-degree linkedin connection, either.

But the hardest ones were ingrained in my various identities. Identities I had built for 30 years of practice, identities I had earned from friends and neighbors (and that would make me smile with pride when I heard one of them reinforcing it), identities I got from chasing promotions at work (the bench boy knows that one), identities carved from pain or shared grief. The list was long and I was not ready to tackle them all.

And I still don't see them all as new ones come to the surface.

Those identities didn't keep me on the chair. They kept me in the masks Acharya Jonas described in a recent series of podcasts.

While I had not decided to put on hold a long-time career of martial arts instructor, I was often drawn to meetings and discussions about the art even though I had not had the time to touch the hands of a student in several months. Whenever friends complimented my handyman skills, I'd feel lost in front of endless unfinished projects scattered around my garage or on top of my workshop bench (which, by the way, stayed unfinished until my dad came to visit and got it done in a couple of days).

The target was never the art. Ving Tsun built most of the good parts of me, and what I wrote in "Silent Pillars" still stands. What had to go was the costume: playing the instructor instead of being one.

It all felt like a house of cards. And how could I even pretend to be compassionate and help others when I could barely hold my own? It would feel like putting a charity donation on a credit card close to maxed out.

## High Energy Expansion

The class that defined compassion as high energy went on to explain how it expands.

So far, that energy mostly reaches the people closest to me. My wife can get out of her car without tripping over the compressor that lived on my garage floor.

A neighbor was going to throw it away because it was broken. I was not. I kept it — and the belief I could fix it — for over two years, and in all that time it collected dust instead of compassion.
