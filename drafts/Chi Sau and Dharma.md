# The More I Won Over My Wife, The More I Lost Her

I could define Dharma, quote it, even write it in Sanskrit. But it remained an idea I held, not something that naturally surfaced in my movement and voice.

Then I remembered something Sifu Leo wrote about Chi Sau. I have lived the same moment more times than I can count.

> We had been practicing Chi Sau for hours. I was doing well, gaining ground, pressing, landing some hits. At one point I felt a clear opening. Everything in me shouted: “Now! Just go all in and finish it.” I went in hard. The result was immediate—and disastrous. I lost the center, broke structure, arms crossing. Completely vulnerable. My Kung Fu brother simply yielded, redirected, and left me out of position. No violence needed.
>
> Later, over tea, Moy Yat asked why I had advanced that way. I said I thought the advantage was mine. He answered: “You didn’t lose because you attacked. You lost because you didn’t think of Faan Sau.”

Faan Sau is not a technique for winning.  
It is a way of moving that keeps the relationship alive.

Not the movement.  
Not the fight.  
The relationship.

Too little energy and the exchange dies.  
Too much and it collapses under its own force.

That is the measure.

## Dead Hands

Chi Sau is the laboratory of Ving Tsun. A controlled place where two people can investigate, in slow motion and endless variation, the split second of contact that would happen in a real fight. Each turn of the hands is an experiment: what works, what collapses, what the other person’s energy and intent actually feel like.

It’s easy to turn that laboratory into a performance drill—circling hands, set pieces, the Pak Sau I watched Bruce Lee do a hundred times. I’ve done all of it. The Amazon training rings, the “invincible” sequences I used on Si Dais until someone better showed them the real thing. All of it produces the same result: a dead relationship.

The hands keep moving, but nothing is alive between them.

A Kung Fu brother once called that kind of Chi Sau “intravaginal masturbation.” Two people going through the motions—circling, striking, defending—yet each somehow alone.

Over time, Chi Sau stopped being a learning tool and became a fun chore on the way to more exciting practices. It was never meant to be that. The exercise was designed to build the exact kind of relationship two people need before they go all out. Lack of attention, speed, and too much self-confidence slowly emptied it. Energy remained. Relevance and respect disappeared.

I watched the same erosion happen in other parts of my life—especially in conversations with the person I had spent the most years with. Only this time there was no Sifu watching. Not until much later, when I started studying Vedanta.

## Empty Talk

I had tools for conversation. Good ones. Fierce Conversations was one of the most effective. At work they worked. At home they felt wrong—forceful, slightly bitter, out of place between husband and wife.

I could close an argument. What I lacked was measure. Something like Faan Sau: a way of moving that kept the relationship alive while the words were still being exchanged.

Without it I kept winning. And the more I won, the more distance I created. I only noticed the cost later, when the conversations had grown thin and I could see the fatigue on my wife’s face. She told me she was tired of discussing things with me because I was always out-reasoning her instead of listening. I could feel her body tighten whenever we argued. I knew I was causing it.

## Dharma

What I lacked had a name.

In class Acharya Jonas offered one meaning of Dharma: what is harmonious to do in the moment. Not the correct opinion. Not the winning move. What sustains the exchange instead of ending it.

It is the same logic as Faan Sau.

Measure is not passivity. It is the capacity to know when to apply force, when to hold back, and when to receive. Too little contact and the relationship dies. Too much and it collapses. Harmony is not the absence of pressure. It is pressure used with accuracy.

I began to notice the difference in real conversations. Moments when pressing forward was right. Moments when the only correct move was to stop, accept what she was saying, and receive it fully. The cost of getting it wrong had already been named more than once:

“Please do not turn breakfast with the neighbors into a debate.”  
“I’m not beating around the bush. I need time to think about what I’m going to say to you.”  
"I feel like I’m always negotiating with you.”

I had been treating conversation the way I used to treat Chi Sau: as something to win. The tools were sharp. The cost was the relationship itself.

What Sifu did for my Chi Sau, Acharya began to do for these conversations. After so many years I had started to take them for granted. I am only now learning to treat them as a living exchange that has to be kept alive.

The conversation is the relationship. That line from Fierce Conversations finally makes sense.

I am still learning the measure. It is not always clear when to push and when to receive, when it is clarity and when it is only ego. The difference is that I can feel the cost more quickly now.

This is only one meaning of Dharma. There are others. For now, this is the one that is changing how I speak.

If you asked my wife, she would say I am inch-worming toward better conversations.

Now I have to notice which conversations I still enter ready to win.
