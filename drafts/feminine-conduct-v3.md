# Feminine Conduct
## Why Ancient Rulers Valued Acting Second — And Why Modern Hustle Gets It Backwards

“All that exists in the world of experiences vanishes the moment it is questioned.”

I wish I had heard that line from Acharya Jonas years ago.

Instead I was told: no pain no gain, lease the German car you can’t really afford, chase that 800 credit score like it’s spiritual enlightenment, work hard and recover never. Crush your first 90 days or get quietly replaced, optimize your life until there’s nothing left to optimize, collect 500 LinkedIn connections you’ll never message, and post the highlight reel of a life you’re too tired to live.

That advice didn’t make me stronger. It left me exhausted — and strangely empty even when I was “winning.”

The light on my path came from my Si Fu, Leo. During late night discussions at the Mo Goon he introduced me to an old Chinese text that described something called *feminine conduct* — the strategic choice to fully receive a situation before deciding how to act.

## The Yellow Emperor

The text my Si Fu pointed me to is part of the *Huangdi Sijing* — the Four Political Treatises of the Yellow Emperor, a collection of silk manuscripts discovered in 1973 that had been buried for over two thousand years. Written in the voice of the legendary Yellow Emperor, the texts lay out a very specific philosophy of rulership. Among its most striking ideas is the distinction between two fundamental modes of conduct: masculine and feminine.

Masculine conduct is aggressive, overt, and impatient. It wants to initiate, dominate, and push forward. It seeks quick wins and visible strength. Feminine conduct, by contrast, is modest, yielding, and patient. It prefers to stand behind rather than in front, to wait rather than to charge. The texts are clear about which one leads to lasting success:

> “It is called the accumulation of disasters to achieve one success after another by persisting in masculine conduct… On the contrary, it is called accumulation of virtues to suffer losses by persisting in feminine conduct. Be cautious and do not give it up in this case, for great gains will be secured in the future.”

The strategy is not to remain passive forever. It is to adopt a receptive, low-profile posture as the default — to wait for the opponent’s strength to weaken or for the right conditions to appear — and only then move decisively.

This is the key nuance the texts insist on. Feminine conduct is not the same as never fighting. It is a way of preparing for the fight so that when you do act, you are not exhausting yourself or creating unnecessary enemies. As the text puts it:

> “Those who initiate contentions suffer misfortune. However, those who do not engage in struggle will have no achievement.”

In other words: don’t start the fight. But don’t refuse to engage when the conditions are right either.

The constant cultural pressure to be the one who initiates, who pushes, who “gets after it” — that was masculine conduct. And it was exhausting me.

I’m not pretending an ancient text hands me a perfect map for my current life. What draws me is the quieter pattern that keeps showing up across traditions — and how it’s slowly reshaping how I move. This is my learning, not a perfect map. I hope you’ll stay with the story anyway.

That same pattern shows up in the art I practice. Ving Tsun is named after its founder, Yim Ving Tsun — a woman in a tradition almost entirely shaped by men. That detail is not accidental. It points to the same principle we’ve been exploring: the power that comes from receiving and redirecting force rather than meeting it head-on.

Over the years I took part in so many events with my Ving Tsun family and I kept noticing the same thing: how both my Si Mo (Si Fu’s wife) and my Si Tai Helen Moy (Si Gung Moy Yat’s wife) work behind the scenes. Whereas I’d jump to “help” during Yam Cha — the traditional Chinese brunch where the whole family gathers — and clumsily serve tea to my Si Fu or brothers, I’d watch how my Si Mo would read the room with surgical precision and time her interventions perfectly. How she’d give a cue when to act and when to speak, keeping me from drawing the wrong kind of attention.

Stories circulated about Si Tai too. When a young man arrived from Brazil to learn Ving Tsun from her husband — without sharing a common language — she took him in. Through gestures of care and attention, she helped shape him into one of the art’s Grandmasters. That man was Leo Imamura, my Si Fu.

My Ving Tsun brother Natan Rosa tells a story from the opposite direction. During a trip to New York, Si Fu Leo assigned him to prepare tea each morning for Si Gung Moy Yat at the Mo Goon. Natan brought the tea. Si Gung didn’t touch it. Day after day — no complaint, no correction, no instruction. Finally Natan noticed the tea served at restaurants was lighter than his. He’d been brewing it too dark. He adjusted. Si Gung drank it without a word.

That’s feminine conduct in teaching: not correcting, not commanding — letting the student see the gap for himself.

As with Siu Nim Tau, it took practice, observation, and patience. It took years before I could feel how timing, energy, positioning, and distance operate in real life — not just in forms or simulated fights. Slowly, my body started staying relaxed. My mind chose to wait — to read the room, to see how everyone moved before I stepped in.

After years of practicing and studying Ving Tsun with this in mind, it was Vedanta that finally gave me the cue to act and a real target: my own ego.

## Echoes in Other Traditions

Something similar appears in the life of Śāradā Devī, the Holy Mother of the Ramakrishna tradition. She didn’t teach through force or public authority. Instead, she received everyone who came to her — the good and the difficult alike — with the simple statement: “I am the mother of whoever is good and also of whoever is bad.” Her power was not in imposing her will, but in her capacity to hold space without judgment. In her kitchen she turned ordinary acts into spiritual practice, showing that feminine conduct is not withdrawal from the world, but a way of moving through it with receptivity and care.

I didn’t know about her until recently. But I recognized the same pattern in my Vedanta classroom.

Sanskrit came at me like a wall I had to climb. Unfamiliar script, unfamiliar sounds, hundreds of nouns with genders that made no sense to me. Hundreds of names, dozens of mantras, the pressure to get every vowel length and articulation right. My mind wasn’t just learning a language; it was racing to prove something. Mastery. Progress. Worthiness. The disguise was subtle: I want to learn. The reality was harder to face: I want to be good at this. I want to be at the top.

During tests, I’d stand with eyes closed, ready to recite in front of the instructor. The mantra I’d memorized hours before would vanish. Silence stretches differently when you’re being watched. After each failure, the instructor’s response was always the same: patient silence. “Just try again.” No frustration. No correction delivered with disappointment. Just the next chance. Over and over.

It took months to understand what was actually happening. They weren’t just teaching me Sanskrit phonemes. They were embodying the same feminine conduct I’d watched in Si Mo and heard about from Natan. Receive the situation fully. Don’t rush to fill the gap. Let me see the error myself and correct it.

The final test was different. Hundreds of words to memorize, grammar rules, vocabulary. A blank piece of paper was for the grammar test what silence was for mantras. Only this time there was no panic. I recognized what had been unfolding in front of me for months. The instructors weren’t guarding their gatekeeping authority. They weren’t fixated on whether I got every answer right or wrong. They cared about the effort I was putting in, my dedication.

I realized this wasn’t a job interview. I had chosen it, and they were there to walk with me. The blank sheet of paper was just paper. And the words came — not perfectly, but without panic. The grammar and vocabulary didn’t disappear because I wasn’t grasping for them anymore. I was receiving them.

That’s the lesson the instructors taught without naming it: you can’t force the mantras to stay. You can only make room for them to appear.

Si Fu, Si Mo, Si Tai, Si Gung, Śāradā Devī, and my Vedanta instructors. They all embodied feminine conduct. And slowly, I started reaching for the teapot less.

I no longer feel the urge to optimize my day. I let posts come and go on my LinkedIn feed without the need to collect 500 connections. I smile when things get difficult, as if I’m inviting the answer to come forward. I pause and let others finish their sentences. I’m all ears. I watch. I receive before I deliver.

The mantras weren’t the point. They were just the training ground.

I spent years trying to be the one who initiates — the guy with the teapot, the connection, the perfect score, charging ahead during the meeting. If you’re tired of that race, here’s a question to sit with:

**Where are you grasping right now? What would happen if you stopped and waited?**

Next time the urge hits — to jump in, to fix it, to be the one with the teapot, to optimize the moment — try waiting one deliberate beat. Let the situation arrive fully before you move. See what becomes visible when you stop reaching for the answer.
