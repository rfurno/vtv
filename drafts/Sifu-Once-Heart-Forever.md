# Sifu Once, Heart Forever
# Why Both Jonas and Sifu Leo Refuse to Let You Copy-Paste Wisdom

Sifu Leo showed me the first section of Siu Nim Tau **exactly once**.

Just once.

Then he went quiet and started poking, correcting, and testing me with his hands until my brain had no choice but to actually do the work. No perfect model to imitate forever. No endless repetition while I zoned out.

Professor Jonas plays the exact same game in Vedānta. He refuses to let you turn “tat tvam asi” into nice Instagram decoration. The words aren’t allowed to stay comfortable in your head — they have to move in, cause trouble, and eventually live in your body.

## The Little Idea Meets Sadhana

I thought I was being clever.

I was driving to work and decided to “catch up” on a missed Vedanta class. So I played the recording (eyes on the road, of course) and let Acharya’s voice fill the car.

Every few minutes I’d hear a catchy phrase and think, *“Oh this is good. I can tell my wife about this one and sound deep.”*

Months went by like this. I collected beautiful little ideas and dropped impressive lines in conversation.

My wife — my most attentive and honest observer — said nothing about any visible change in me.

Then one day the real meaning of something Acharya said finally landed:

> “Há a necessidade de confiança e de entrega ao processo de estudo.”

*There is a need for trust and surrender to the process of study.*

And it hit me like a slow punch:

I had the recording playing… while half my attention was on the road.  
That’s not **confiança**.

I had seven full days to watch the class properly, sitting down, fully engaged.  
I didn’t do it.  
That’s not **entrega**.

I was consuming knowledge.  
I wasn’t practicing it.

## Understanding Instead of the Perfect Imitation

The second time Sifu Leo was about to show me the next part of Siu Nim Tau, he looked at me and said, “Pay attention.”

This time I actually understood what he meant.

I had to be fully present. I had to learn where to look, what mattered, and how to *feel* the movement instead of just copying it.

He kept reminding me that real Ving Tsun wasn’t about building a perfect copy of him. It followed a different kind of logic — one where I had to slowly develop my own version. My body needed to respond in the moment, not just repeat what looked good in the mirror.

For years, almost everything else in my life had been about imitation: copy the model, repeat the drill, get it looking right. But if I wanted to actually *use* Ving Tsun when something unexpected happened, I couldn’t just be a good photocopy of my Sifu or my senior kung fu brothers and sisters.

I had to grow my own.

In the old way — let’s call it **model logic** — you learn by watching and repeating.  
In the **logic of self-development**, real knowledge only emerges when you stop trying to imitate and start understanding.

And that process is much slower, much more uncomfortable… and far more valuable.

That memory hit me again while listening to Acharya. He was describing the same idea, just using different words: Śravaṇa, Manana, and Nididhyāsana.

I quietly admitted to myself how badly I was doing all three.

I’m still terrible at them. My ego is especially proud of how good I am at multitasking.

But at least now I can see the gap between collecting clever ideas and actually doing the work.

## When the Lesson Doesn't Get to the Heart, It Comes Out of the Mouth

*Inspired by Swami Dayananda Saraswati*

A few months later, I hung up from a FaceTime call with my mother. My wife looked at me with a small, surprised smile.

“You handled that really well,” she said.

She wasn’t used to seeing me respond to stress that calmly. For once, I hadn’t launched into a Vedanta explanation. I had simply *done* it — or at least come closer than usual.

Something had quietly shifted. The lessons were no longer just nice ideas I collected. They were starting to become my own words and actions.

That “something” isn’t magic. It’s just what happens when you finally stop trying to copy-paste wisdom and let it do its slow, uncomfortable work.

## Sifu once, Acharya once — Heart forever.

Both Sifu Leo and Acharya Jonas refuse to let me turn their teaching into performance.

They showed me the moves once, said the words once, and then stepped back — forcing me to either do the real work or keep pretending.

I’m still mostly pretending. But every once in a while, the gap between “knowing” and “being” gets a little smaller. And my wife usually notices before I do.

That’s enough reason to keep showing up — even when it’s slow, repetitive, and deeply humbling.

Because real transmission doesn’t happen in the notebook or the mirror.  
It happens in the body, in the choices, and in the quiet moments when no one is watching.

Sifu once, Acharya once — Heart forever.
