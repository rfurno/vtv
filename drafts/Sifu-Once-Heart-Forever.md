# Sifu Once, Heart Forever
# Why Both Jonas and Sifu Leo Refuse to Let You Copy-Paste Wisdom

Sifu Leo showed me the first section of Siu Nim Tau **exactly once**.

Just once.

Then he went quiet and started poking, correcting, and testing me with his hands until my brain had no choice but to actually do the work. No perfect model to imitate forever. No endless repetition while I zoned out.

Professor Jonas plays the exact same game in Vedānta. He refuses to let you turn “tat tvam asi” into nice Instagram decoration. The words are not allowed to stay comfortable in your head — they have to move in, cause trouble, and eventually become yours and live in your body.


## The Little Idea Meets Sadhana

I thought I was being clever.

I was driving to work and decided to “catch up” on a missed Vedanta class. So I played the recording (I was **not** watching the video, of course) and let Acharya’s voice fill the car.

Every few minutes I’d hear a catchy phrase and think, *“Oh this is good. I can tell my wife about this one.”*

Months went by like this. I collected beautiful little ideas. I dropped impressive lines in conversation with my wife and friends who also study Vedanta.

My wife — my most attentive and honest observer — said nothing about any visible change in me.

Then one day the real meaning of something Acharya had said finally landed:

> “Há a necessidade de confiança e de entrega ao processo de estudo.”

*There is a need for trust and surrender to the process of study.*

And it hit me like a slow punch:

I had the recording playing… while half my attention was on the road.  
That’s not **confiança** (trust).

I had seven full days to watch the class properly, sitting down, fully engaged like I was actually there.  
I didn’t do it.  
That’s not **entrega** (surrender).

I was consuming knowledge.  
I wasn’t practicing it.

## Understanding Instead of the Perfect Imitation

When Sifu Leo was about to show me the next part of Siu Nim Tau, he looked at me and said, “Pay attention.”

This time I actually understood what he meant.

I had to be fully present. I had to learn where to look, what actually mattered, and how to feel the movement instead of just copying it.

He kept reminding me that real Ving Tsun wasn’t about building a perfect copy of him. It followed a different kind of logic — one where I had to slowly develop my own version. My body needed to learn how to respond in the moment, not just repeat what looked good in the mirror.

For years, almost everything else in my life had been about imitation: copy the model, repeat the drill, get it looking right. But if I wanted to actually *use* Ving Tsun when something unexpected happened outside the kwoon, I couldn’t just be a good photocopy of my Sifu or my older kung fu brothers and sisters.

I had to grow my own.

In the old way — let’s call it **model logic** — you learn by watching and repeating.  
In the **logic of self-development**, the real knowledge only emerges when you stop trying to imitate and start understanding.

And that process is much slower, much more uncomfortable… and far more valuable.

That memory hit me again recently while listening to Acharya. I realized he was describing the same idea, just in Vedanta language.

He spoke about three stages — Śravaṇa, Manana, and Nididhyāsana — and I quietly admitted to myself how badly I had been doing all three.

**Śravaṇa** is supposed to mean listening properly to a qualified teacher who actually knows the tradition. Not casually listening to a recording while driving. Not reading random stuff on your own or chatting with friends who are also confused. Real, systematic teaching.

**Manana** means reflecting on what you heard and working through every doubt with that same teacher. I had mostly been doing “mental agreement” — nodding along when it sounded nice, then forgetting it two days later when life got stressful.

**Nididhyāsana** is letting the understanding actually settle in and change how you see things naturally. Not forcing it. Not doing some fancy meditation. Just allowing the knowledge to quietly reorganize your mind.

I’m still terrible at all of them. And my ego is great at reminding me that I'm great at multitasking.

But at least now I can see the gap between “collecting clever ideas” and actually doing the work.

## When the Lesson Doesn't Get to the Heart, It Comes Out of the Mouth

A few months later, I hung up from a FaceTime call with my mother. My wife looked at me with a small, surprised smile.

“You handled that really well,” she said.

She wasn’t used to seeing me respond to stress that calmly. For once, I hadn’t launched into an explanation about Vedanta. I had simply *done* it — or at least gotten closer than usual.

Something had quietly shifted. The lessons were no longer just clever ideas I collected. They were starting to become my own words and actions.

That “something” isn’t magic. It’s just what happens when you finally stop trying to copy-paste wisdom and let it do its slow, uncomfortable work.

## Sifu Once, Heart Forever

Both Sifu Leo and Acharya Jonas keep refusing to let me turn their teaching into performance.

They showed me the moves once, said the words once, and then stepped back — forcing me to either do the real work or keep pretending.

I’m still mostly pretending. But every once in a while, the gap between “knowing” and “being” gets a little smaller. And when it does, my wife notices before I do.

That’s enough reason to keep showing up — even when it’s slow, repetitive, and deeply humbling.

Because real transmission doesn’t happen in the notebook or the mirror.  
It happens in the body, in the choices, and in the quiet moments when no one is watching.

Sifu once. Heart forever.
